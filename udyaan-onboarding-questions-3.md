## 1. What's pulling you toward agriculture specifically, and what are you actually willing to risk to build something in it on your own?

**Option A — Personal stake, willing to bet time**
"[Specific connection to agriculture — family, community, direct exposure]. That's why it's this space and not another. I'm willing to put in [specific commitment — e.g., a gap year, evenings and weekends for a year, savings] to see if I can build something real in it, alone."

**Option B — A gap I can see clearly, willing to bet skill**
"I don't have a personal history in agriculture, but I can see a specific, under-addressed gap: [problem area]. What I'm risking isn't sentimental attachment — it's my time and [specific skill], on a bet that this gap is real and solvable."

**Option C — Genuine curiosity, willing to test conviction**
"I'm drawn to agriculture because [specific reason — e.g., it's one of the few sectors where a small idea can have outsized real-world impact], but I haven't proven to myself yet that the interest holds up under pressure. I'm willing to spend real time finding out before I call it conviction."

**Option D — A practical opportunity I want to commit to conditionally**
"[Problem area] looked like a real opportunity when I looked into it, and agriculture is where I happened to find it. I'm willing to commit fully once I've done enough digging to trust the problem — Udyaan is where I want to do that digging."

Recommended: Option A if genuinely true — a real personal stake plus a concrete commitment is the strongest signal that you'll stay in it when it gets hard. Option C is the most honest fallback if you don't have that history; naming the specific reason for the pull matters more than claiming a connection you don't have.

## 2. Describe a time you had to make a call on your own, with no one to check it against. What happened?

**Option A — It worked out, and I'd do it again**
"[Situation] — I had to decide [decision] with no one to run it by. I went with [choice] based on [reasoning], and it worked out because [result]. I'd trust that same judgment again."

**Option B — It didn't fully work, and I learned from it**
"[Situation] — I made the call alone, it didn't go the way I expected, and I only found out why afterward: [what you learned]. Being wrong alone, and owning it, taught me more than being right with backup would have."

**Option C — I sought out a proxy for judgment**
"I didn't have a mentor or team, so I built my own way of checking myself — [e.g., a specific person I called, a rule I applied, data I looked at] — before committing. It wasn't a real second opinion, but it kept me honest."

**Option D — I hesitated, and that was the real lesson**
"[Situation] — I froze or delayed the decision longer than I should have because no one was there to validate it. What I took from that is [specific insight about your own decision-making under isolation]."

Recommended: Option B or D, whichever is genuinely true — a specific story about being wrong or stuck alone is more convincing than a clean success story.

## 3. Tell us about a setback or rejection in something you were building or pursuing. How did you respond, and did you continue?

**Option A — I adjusted the approach and kept going**
"[Setback] happened when I was working on [what you were building]. Instead of dropping it, I changed [specific thing] and continued. It taught me that the setback was about the method, not the goal."

**Option B — I paused, reconsidered, and came back differently**
"After [setback], I stepped away from it for a while rather than pushing through immediately. When I came back, I approached it as [different framing/approach], which worked better than forcing the original plan."

**Option C — I let it end, and that was the right call**
"[Setback] made it clear that [specific reason] wasn't going to work. I chose to stop rather than keep investing in something the evidence didn't support — and I still think that was the right decision, not a failure of will."

**Option D — I'm still in it, unresolved**
"I'm currently dealing with [setback] in [what you're building], and I haven't fully solved it yet. I'm including this because I want to be honest about where I actually am, not just tell a story with a tidy ending."

Recommended: Whichever is true. Option C is underused but valuable if genuine — knowing when to stop is as important a solopreneur skill as persistence.

## 4. How would you find out if an agri idea is actually worth building, before you spend months on it?

**Option A — Talk to the people who'd use it first**
"Before building anything, I'd go talk directly to [farmers/the specific user group] about the problem — not to pitch them a solution, but to check whether the problem is as real and painful as I think it is."

**Option B — Build the smallest possible version and test it**
"I'd skip the research phase and build a rough, minimal version of the idea as fast as possible, then get it in front of real users to see if they actually use it — rather than asking them hypothetically."

**Option C — Look for existing evidence first**
"I'd start by checking whether anyone else has tried this — competitors, failed attempts, adjacent solutions — to understand why it worked or didn't, before assuming my version would be different."

**Option D — Use my own direct experience as the first signal**
"Because of [your personal/family/community connection to agriculture], I've already seen this problem up close. I'd validate further with others, but my starting point is that I've lived close enough to it to trust the instinct — and then go test it."

Recommended: Option A, unless you have a genuine personal connection (then D is stronger). Talking to real users before building is the most common gap in first-time founders.

## 5. Picture yourself six months into building this alone. What have you actually built, and what would convince you it's worth continuing?

**Option A — A tested idea with early signal**
"By six months, I'd want to have [specific version of the idea] in front of real [farmers/users], with [specific signal — e.g., a handful of repeat users, someone willing to pay]. That signal, even small, is what would convince me to keep going rather than switch problems."

**Option B — A working prototype, even if rough**
"I'd rather have something ugly but functional than a polished plan — by six months I want a version people can actually use, not just describe. If it's being used, even by a few people, that's proof enough for me to continue."

**Option C — Clarity on the problem, even without a product yet**
"I might not have a built product yet, but I'd want to have talked to enough [farmers/users] to know exactly which version of the problem is worth solving. For me, that clarity — knowing precisely what to build and for whom — is the real six-month milestone."

**Option D — Evidence I was wrong, and a better direction because of it**
"I'd expect my first idea to be at least partly wrong. What would convince me to continue isn't that the original idea worked — it's that I found out fast where it broke, and used that to land on a sharper version worth pursuing."

Recommended: Option A or B if you already have a specific idea in mind — concreteness here matters more than ambition. Option D is a strong choice if you don't have a fixed idea yet, since it shows you're planning for iteration rather than betting everything on being right the first time.
