Digital Maturity Index

Why the index is scored from evidence rather than a questionnaire, and how that choice lets one model extend across many organisations.

Framework dmi-1.0 Status Implemented Date 27 July 2026

The core design decision

Most digital maturity models are questionnaires. Someone sits down once a year and rates their organisation against a rubric. Three things reliably go wrong:

  • Scores drift upward. Self-rating rewards optimism, and nobody wants to record that their unit is Level 1.
  • Nothing is comparable. Two organisations answering the same question apply different standards, so cross-organisation figures are noise.
  • The score can be improved without the organisation improving. Re-rating is free; changing practice is not.
The decision

Score the organisation from what it has actually done in the platform, never from what it says about itself. Every dimension is computed from records that already exist — projects, weekly updates, meetings, action items, results-chain entries, connected workspaces.

The consequence is the point: the number cannot be talked up. It only moves when behaviour changes. If Process Discipline is 61, the way to make it 75 is to file the missing weekly reports and close the overdue action items. There is no form to re-submit.

A second consequence is that the score is explainable. Every dimension exposes the raw counts it was derived from, so "why 42?" always has an answer an admin can check. This matters more than precision: a maturity score people do not trust is a score people ignore.

How a score is built

Six dimensions, each scored 0–100 from named signals, then combined as a weighted mean. Weights encode what the programme actually cares about — governance, discipline and measurement carry the most, because they are what turn activity into managed work.

DimensionWeightScored from
Strategy & Governance20%Projects with a full brief, a deadline, and an assigned team
Process Discipline20%Weekly reporting rate over the last 8 closed weeks, action-item closure rate, overdue backlog
Collaboration & Tooling15%Projects with a shared workspace, projects holding meetings, meetings with minutes recorded
Data & Measurement20%Impact entries carrying a measured value and a target; projects measuring anything at all
Outcome Orientation15%Results-chain completeness; projects reaching outcomes or impact rather than stopping at outputs
People & Capability10%Coverage of leadership / mentorship / delivery roles; share of people who left any trace
Composite100%Weighted mean, re-normalised over scored dimensions only

Extending across organisations

The index is only worth building if it works for the tenth organisation as well as the first. Four choices make that true.

1. One versioned ruler

The framework lives in code as dmi-1.0 — dimensions, weights and level bands are fixed for a version. Every organisation is scored identically, which is the only thing that makes a cross-organisation benchmark mean anything.

Deliberate constraint

Per-organisation tailoring happens by marking a dimension not applicable — never by re-weighting. Allowing custom weights would let an organisation score well simply by discounting whatever it is worst at, which destroys both comparability and honesty.

2. A young organisation is measured on what it has started

A dimension with no evidence is flagged applicable = false and excluded, and the remaining weights are re-normalised. An organisation that has not begun measuring impact is not scored zero on Data & Measurement and shamed for it — that dimension simply does not count yet. Each result carries a coverage figure so a reader knows how much of the framework the score actually rests on.

3. Benchmarking is part of the model, not a report bolted on

A score is only useful once you know whether it is good. The benchmark endpoint scores every organisation on the same version and returns the cohort average, the caller's percentile, per-dimension cohort averages, and a leaderboard. In the interface, each dimension bar carries a tick showing where the cohort sits, so you read your score and its context together.

Organisations with no projects and no people are skipped, so an empty shell tenant cannot drag the cohort average toward zero.

4. Snapshots survive a framework change

Dimension results are stored as JSON against the framework version that produced them, rather than as a fixed child table. A future dmi-2.0 can add, remove or redefine dimensions without a migration, and history captured under 1.0 stays readable and correctly labelled.

Trade-offs worth remembering

  • It measures platform behaviour, not the organisation entire. Work done well but recorded elsewhere is invisible to the index. This is an accepted cost of refusing self-assessment; it also creates a healthy incentive to keep records in one place.
  • Weights are a judgement, not a discovery. The 20/20/15/20/15/10 split encodes a view about what matters. It should be revisited with evidence once enough organisations are scored — and revisiting it means publishing dmi-2.0, not quietly editing 1.0.
  • Small organisations move sharply. With one project, a single missing deadline swings Governance by a third. Coverage is reported alongside the score partly to signal this.
  • No AI in the scoring path. Scoring is deterministic arithmetic so the same records always produce the same number and any figure can be reproduced by hand. The AI Advisor interprets; the index counts.
